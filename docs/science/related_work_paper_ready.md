# Proposed replacement for Section 2 — Related Work

This version is written for direct integration into the English manuscript. It deliberately distinguishes retrospective HR-informed reconstruction from genuine prospective prediction and frames the contribution as incremental to the closest published trail-running digital-twin work.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Trail-running performance prediction

Trail-running performance has commonly been studied through athlete-level physiological and anthropometric determinants. A systematic review by [de Waal et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2020-0812) found that maximal aerobic capacity, aerobic speed, metabolic thresholds, running economy, and body composition were the variables most often associated with performance, but also emphasized the small evidence base and the heterogeneity of race distances, gradients, surfaces, and study protocols. The classical endurance-running triad—maximal oxygen uptake, the sustainable fraction of maximal oxygen uptake, and running economy—is therefore relevant but insufficiently terrain-specific. In nine trained runners completing a 27-km trail race, [Ehrström et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001467) found that local muscular endurance and maximal oxygen uptake were more informative than several conventional variables, motivating gradient-specific economy and neuromuscular assessment. [Alvero-Cruz et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2019.01306) combined maximal oxygen uptake and fat mass to explain short-trail race time, whereas [Scheer et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2018-0390) showed that laboratory indicators gained substantial explanatory power when the previous year's time on the same race was added. Across races of different lengths, [Pastor et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2021-0362) likewise reported that aerobic capacity was a major determinant, with other factors becoming distance dependent. These studies explain between-runner differences in a race-level outcome, but they do not reconstruct how an individual runner's speed changes continuously with route demand and internal state.

Field studies further show that a trail race cannot be reduced to a single physiological capacity. During a 65-km mountain ultramarathon, [Fornasiero et al. (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28869746/) characterized intensity relative to ventilatory thresholds and summarized whole-race load with TRIMP. During a shorter ecological trial, [Genitrini et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1406824) observed slower running, longer contact time, greater duty factor, shorter stride length, and terrain-dependent kinematic changes in the later stages. Such results support an explicit time-varying fatigue state, but neither study turns the observed state changes into an externally evaluated race-time model.

A separate literature predicts performance statistically from race history or in-race checkpoints. [Emig and Peltonen (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-18737-6) inferred individualized aerobic-power and endurance indices from approximately 1.6 million real-world running activities, obtaining accurate predictions across standard road-race distances from distance and duration records. For trail running, the TRAP framework of [Fogliato et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1515/jqas-2020-0013) combines ITRA history, runner characteristics, terrain, and checkpoint information to estimate the probability of reaching the next checkpoint and its passage time. More recently, [Gutiérrez et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports13110385) used the first third of a race, terrain-weighted section times, pacing variability, and checkpoint rank to explain more than 95% of total-time variance in one multi-edition event. These are useful operational models, but their outputs answer a different question: they condition on historical performance or on partial completion of the target race and do not expose a physiological mechanism linking momentary effort, terrain, and fatigue.

### 2.2 Route energetics and physiology-based digital twins

Mechanistic trail models generally start from the energetic cost of gradient. [Minetti et al. (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01177.2001) quantified the metabolic cost of walking and running across extreme positive and negative treadmill slopes, providing the basis for many grade-adjusted pace transformations. Transfer to real trails is nevertheless imperfect. In matched uphill trials, [Nicot et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2021.1995507) measured a 10.5% higher oxygen cost on trail than on a treadmill and substantially greater medio-lateral foot-acceleration magnitude and variability, showing that slope alone does not represent surface technicality. Complementing this evidence, [Holler and Jaén-Carrillo (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2025.112892) extended a parametric mechanical-power model with collision losses, elastic-energy storage, force-rate, upper-body, slope, and uneven-terrain terms. This produces an interpretable estimate of route demand, but it is not itself a validated model of long-duration physiological fatigue or finish time.

Physiology-based digital twins offer a complementary route to individualization. [Boillet et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-56042-0) reinterpreted a three-compartment Margaria–Morton model using measured physiological parameters and applied it to four national-level cyclists. The model reproduced short-to-medium-duration power, oxygen-kinetic, and metabolite behaviors, but requires several laboratory tests and was developed for near-maximal efforts shorter than approximately 15–20 min rather than prolonged, terrain-varying trail races.

The closest predecessor to the present study is the physics-based trail-running digital twin of [Jaén-Carrillo and Pattis (2026)](https://doi.org/10.3390/s26123731). In a single-athlete proof of concept across 13 races, their model combined grade-adjusted pace or Minetti-type energetics, altitude, a TRIMP-derived load term, and pacing decay. Leave-one-race-out evaluation produced an activity-level MAE of 18.2 min, MAPE of 11.1%, and \(R^2=0.864\). That study establishes the feasibility of an interpretable, athlete-specific trail digital twin and is therefore the appropriate direct baseline. The present work differs by isolating the incremental information in the continuously observed heart-rate-reserve trajectory and by evaluating a model ladder at both activity and terrain-segment levels. It should consequently be presented as an extension and empirical decomposition of this modeling family, rather than as the first trail-running digital twin.

### 2.3 Heart rate as an internal-state signal

Heart-rate reserve (HRR) provides a practical individualized normalization. Under controlled incremental exercise, [Swain and Leutholtz (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-199703000-00018) showed that percentage HRR corresponds more closely to percentage oxygen-uptake reserve than to percentage maximal oxygen uptake. HR-derived training-load models also provide a precedent for temporal accumulation. Banister's TRIMP weights exercise duration by relative heart rate, while the individualized formulation of [Manzi et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181a6a959) derives the weighting function from each runner's heart-rate–blood-lactate relationship. Importantly, these formulations were designed primarily to summarize session load and relate training over days or weeks to adaptation. Fornasiero et al. used TRIMP to quantify the total load of one ultramarathon, but this does not by itself validate cumulative TRIMP as a within-race state variable that causally reduces speed.

Several studies caution against treating heart rate as a direct, instantaneous measurement of mechanical or metabolic effort on variable terrain. In a 7-km hilly trail trial, [Born et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2016-0101) observed similar uphill and downhill percentages of maximal heart rate despite a much larger difference in oxygen uptake; muscle oxygenation tracked the slope-induced changes more closely. [Lemire et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2020.06.004) likewise found slope-specific determinants of uphill and downhill endurance performance. Over longer durations, cardiovascular drift, temperature, hydration, and altitude can change the mapping between heart rate and external work. [Wingo et al. (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1249/01.MSS.0000152731.33450.95) linked cardiovascular drift during exercise in the heat to reduced available maximal oxygen uptake, while [Wehrlin and Hallén (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-005-0081-9) measured declining maximal oxygen uptake and performance with increasing altitude, together with a smaller decline in maximal heart rate. Measurement modality is another potential source of error: during variable-intensity trail running, [Navalta et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0238569) found poor agreement for photoplethysmography devices and recommended an ECG-based chest strap. Conversely, preliminary field-diagnostic evidence is encouraging: in a conference abstract, [Feist et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.36950/2026.2ciss025) predicted laboratory anaerobic-threshold heart rate from a standardized outdoor trail protocol with leave-one-out RMSE of 4.34 bpm. Taken together, these studies justify HRR as an informative but delayed and context-dependent internal-response signal, not as metabolic ground truth.

### 2.4 Research gap and positioning of the present study

The literature therefore leaves a narrow but important intersection insufficiently tested. Route-physics models are interpretable but incompletely represent the athlete's changing internal state; laboratory digital twins are physiologically detailed but burdensome and poorly matched to long trail races; race-history and checkpoint models can be highly predictive but are not pre-race physiological simulations; and HR/TRIMP studies generally summarize intensity or training load rather than coupling a continuous internal signal to segment-wise route demand.

The present study tests whether, within an athlete, continuous HRR explains residual speed variation after grade and altitude correction, and whether a cumulative TRIMP-like state captures additional time-order dependence. Because both variables are derived from heart rate, their effects are treated as predictive components rather than proof of two independently identified physiological mechanisms. We use the term **retrospective HR-informed reconstruction** when the target activity's measured HR trajectory is supplied to the model. We reserve **prospective simulation** for predictions generated without any target-activity observations, using a prescribed HRR scenario. This distinction prevents the accuracy of an HR-informed reconstruction from being interpreted as the accuracy available before a race.

Finally, leave-one-activity-out evaluation in the current single-athlete data set estimates generalization across activities conditional on that athlete; it does not establish transfer to other runners. The contribution of the present proof of concept is therefore (i) an interpretable decomposition of route, instantaneous HRR, and accumulated-load terms; (ii) segment- and activity-level out-of-sample error estimates; and (iii) explicit documentation of the failure boundary between athlete-specific retrospective reconstruction and genuinely prospective prediction. Population-level validity remains a separate hypothesis for multi-athlete, group-aware external evaluation.
